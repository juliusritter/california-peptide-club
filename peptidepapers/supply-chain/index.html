Chapter I

The Supply Chain

Draft, based on my New York Peptides 101 talk (video). Not medical advice. None of this is medical advice.

The molecules have been around for decades. What is changing is the pipes they flow through.

Every vial of BPC-157 in a biohacker’s bathroom fridge, every pen of semaglutide in a soccer mom’s purse, and most of the raw material behind Eli Lilly’s blockbusters trace back, one way or another, to the same place. Almost nobody who uses these compounds could draw the map. That is a mistake. If you want situational awareness on peptides in America, don’t start with the molecules. Start with the supply chain, because every fight that matters in this industry over the next five years, in courtrooms, at the FDA, on Discord, is a fight over who controls one of its pipes.

It all starts in China

The overwhelming majority of the world’s peptide supply, outside the facilities big pharma owns outright, is synthesized in China. There is some capacity in India, some in Estonia, and friends of mine are building American plants that do everything from scratch, but that buildout is nascent. The Chinese manufacturers have spent decades compounding process advantages the way other industries compound capital, and they are now simply the best in the world at making these molecules. So they make most of them.

The FDA has green-lit a set of Chinese labs, originally seven, now down to five, that passed its quality bar. You would expect every serious manufacturer to apply, but language barriers and asymmetric information prevent them from doing so. 99% of the economics happen in the United States, at the point of upsell. The factory in Zhejiang captures a rounding error; the margin lives with whoever bottles, brands, and retails the powder in America. A certification that mostly benefits your American resellers is not worth the paperwork.

Hold that number in your head and the entire market snaps into focus, from the five labs to the gray market’s pricing to the fact that, if you find the right Discord server and have a friend who speaks Chinese, or Google-Translate your way through, you can buy the same peptides for 5 to 10% of what an American research site charges. I’ll come back to that, because I did it.

The gray market is smaller than you think

The first buyers from those Chinese facilities are the research-use-only vendors, the gray market. Research-use-only means compounds without FDA approval: BPC-157, tesamorelin, Epithalon, even GHK-Cu. These vendors source from a Chinese manufacturer, which may itself source from yet another lab, then resell. The value they add is thin: take the active ingredient, maybe run one more cleaning step in the US, and bottle it into tiny vials of lyophilized powder.

The gray is specific. Buying is not a crime. But the website can never say the product is for human consumption, and usually says the opposite, explicitly: “not for human consumption,” “for research use only.” Everyone involved knows you are not running cell cultures at home. For years the mechanics were pure frontier: send crypto, or Venmo some random account, and hope the peptides arrive miraculously. That era is ending: Peptide Sciences, the largest of these sites, recently shut down voluntarily as FDA pressure escalated.

Is the product any good? Mostly yes, for the most boring reason in economics: incentives. A vendor who ships garbage exits the market. But the tail risk is real. I have seen a certificate of analysis come back 0% peptide found. There is a documented case of a buyer who ordered retatrutide, noticed he was getting strangely tan, and learned his vial was actually Melanotan II. So a serious buyer checks three things: right peptide, right amount, and above all purity, because when a label says 98%, the remaining 2% can be anything, and certain synthesis salts are more dangerous than the endotoxins and heavy metals people usually worry about. Third-party testing infrastructure now exists, per vendor and per peptide, and the distributions are really promising: for the good vendors, the medians and even the 25th to 75th percentiles sit inside acceptable ranges.

What recalibrates people is the size of it all: this entire shadow economy is tiny. Research-use-only peptides move maybe 200 to 400 million dollars a year in the US by the last stats I found. Trusted reporting anchors about $100M as a floor; our own sizing puts 2026 retail closer to $0.5B. Take whichever number you like, then set it against branded GLP-1s, running around $55 billion a year in the US alone and pointed at far more by 2030. All the culture, the Discord servers, the TikTok discourse, and financially the gray market is a rounding error on Lilly’s income statement. The culture is upstream of the revenue. That gap is the opportunity, and everyone from PE to big pharma can see it.

The oldest job in pharmacy

The second buyer of Chinese powder is the compounding pharmacy. People treat compounding as an exotic loophole. It is the opposite: compounding pharmacies were the first pharmacies. The original job of a pharmacist was to take a doctor’s prescription and mix it on the spot. Mass manufacturing made that mostly obsolete, yet compounding still accounts for something like 10 to 20% of all medications dispensed, and shrinking.

A compounding pharmacy has the legal ability and the resources to compound peptides from the active pharmaceutical ingredient. The strategic question is where the ingredient comes from, and the answer is also China. People tell me, “Oh, I get my stuff from a compounding pharmacy,” and I have to break it to them that it is possibly the exact same lab shipping to the gray-market site they look down on. The prescription and the pharmacist are real value. The powder often comes off the same production line.

One piece of jargon worth knowing: 503A, the small traditional compounding pharmacies. Right now every M&A and private equity person is circling them, asking whether this is the next HVAC roll-up. The larger 503B outsourcing facilities are insanely capital-intensive to stand up, and people are building those too. When the roll-up capital and the regulatory lawyers show up in the same quarter, you are watching an industry get repriced in real time.

Patent law, not safety

Why doesn’t someone patent BPC-157 and run it through trials? A patent on BPC exists. It is for sale; there is a website. Nobody buys it, because it protects nothing: attach some incidental molecule to BPC that behaves identically in the body and you have, legally, a new molecule. With no durable protection on short peptide chains, no company will fund clinical trials, because there is no commercial prize on the other side. That is the entire reason these compounds live in legal limbo. Patent law, not safety.

The magic number is 40. Chain more than 40 amino acids together and the law calls it a biologic, with roughly 12 years of much stronger protection, extendable. Which brings us to my favorite courtroom drama in the entire industry: Lilly is currently arguing that retatrutide, a 39-amino-acid peptide, should classify as a biologic, because it carries two additional complex strands that are technically peptides of their own, so it should count as 41. They are spending millions on that argument, and they want tirzepatide reclassified too.

They are defending real territory. Retatrutide is the best weight-loss drug the world has ever seen. Ozempic was the first generation, Mounjaro and Zepbound the second, and retatrutide came through phase 3 with results strong enough that new positive side effects surface almost weekly. Tirzepatide and retatrutide made Lilly the first trillion-dollar biotech in history, on top of that ~$55 billion US branded GLP-1 run rate. Lilly has announced $3.5 billion this year for, I think, four new production facilities. And yet I have heard, from someone I trust a lot who was recently on Huberman, that even they source some raw material from China. I cannot verify it, but it tells you how deep the dependency runs.

Pharma plays offense too: campaigns against compounding pharmacies, warning letters landing on individual doctors telling them not to prescribe BPC even under a renamed label. And Lilly has apparently signaled to the compounders and the research-use-only crowd, more or less directly: do what you want on the gray market, but stay away from retatrutide. We will see whether that line holds.

The future of compounding

In the talk this chapter comes from, I told the room to watch two dates: July 23rd and 24th, when the FDA’s compounding advisory committee would reconsider seven peptides stuck in Category 2. Category 2 means one thing: do not compound. (Category 3 just means insufficient data, and it is random enough that I think rosemary is in there.) The meeting happened, and it went further than I expected. The committee voted, against FDA staff recommendations six straight times, to recommend BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax, and Epitalon for the 503A compounding list. BPC-157 passed 8 to 6. Emideltide missed by a single vote. The votes are non-binding, rulemaking is still pending, and as of today none of this is legal to compound, with a second meeting on five more peptides penciled in for early 2027. But the direction is set: the wellness peptides are migrating out of the gray market and toward licensed pharmacies, and the committee’s own logic was that pharmacies, not black markets, should be the ones distributing them. RFK’s HHS says it in plain words: “We created the black market. And it’s a very dangerous black market.” Play the good case forward and you get BPC-157 with a prescription, from a pharmacist, without ever touching a research-use-only website, maybe 3 to 12 months after the FDA finalizes. That still would not make it an FDA-approved drug, and I don’t think that matters much. Approval status was never a clean signal anyway: clomiphene was approved, and enclomiphene, literally a component of clomiphene, never was.

The GLP-1 side of compounding is meanwhile dying on schedule. The Fifth Circuit affirmed the FDA in both shortage cases on August 27, which closes the legal road back to shortage-based mass compounding. Commissioner Makary is not chasing pharmacies one by one; he is choking the API import pipeline, and 55 warning letters hit telehealth companies this year over marketing alone. The clearest scoreboard is Hims & Hers, which, while still selling $49-a-month compounded semaglutide in February, capitulated in March into a Novo distribution deal and agreed to stop mass-marketing compounded GLP-1s. The counterattack has moved to antitrust, Strive Compounding v. Lilly and Novo, pending in Texas.

The experts now split into two clean camps. The bulls, APC’s Scott Brunner (“What authority does FDA have to restrict compounding with a particular API that otherwise meets Congress’ plain language?”) and Empower’s Shaun Noorian (“The system treats compounding like a loophole instead of a lifeline”), plus the private equity buyers circling the 503As, argue compounding survives on statute and personalization. Lilly, Novo, and the clinical skeptics answer that personalization is a fig leaf enforcement will eventually strip away. My read is that both camps are describing the same future: compounding gets smaller in GLP-1s and broader in everything else, more regulated, and more politically contested than at any point in its history.

And there is a wilder branch on this tree: RFK wants peptides categorized as supplements. Play that one forward and the research-use-only space probably sees a race to the bottom, compounding pharmacies take over on brand and reputation, and then come the CPG products: chocolates, gummies, powders, GLPs and BPCs for the masses. That future already showed up at my house. A founder visiting from Canada handed me a piece of tirzepatide chocolate. In theory the gut should destroy the peptide; in practice the lipids seem protective, and I could feel the effect.

Going direct

I told you where the economics live: the 99% American markup. So I tested it. I tried a lot of different suppliers, partly out of scientific curiosity, partly because a serious peptide stack runs $600 or $700 a month retail, which is not sustainable.

Right now I pay $10 for a 10-milligram vial of retatrutide that costs about $90 on a research-use-only site. Same molecule, probably a similar lab, one-ninth the price. The trade is that you absorb the entire risk stack yourself. On my first order the supplier took my roughly $300, claimed their account got banned, and said the money never arrived; my Chinese roommate had to go to war in the negotiation before they shipped. The vials are at the testing lab now. Also, the sales rep’s profile photo was 100% AI generated. There is no way Anna from China looks like that.

That is the trade the whole supply chain offers, at every altitude. Buy from Lilly: full price, full accountability, $349 a month cash-pay semaglutide, genuinely reasonable against what it used to cost. Buy from a compounding pharmacy: a prescription, a pharmacist, and Chinese powder. Buy gray: cheaper, mostly clean, caveat emptor. Go direct to the source: 90% off, and you had better be sending samples to a lab.

We are early in the migration of health decisions away from the pharma small-molecule complex and into individual hands. The infrastructure is messy, legally ambiguous, and running on Venmo and Discord. It is also compounding, in every sense, faster than the people regulating it can move. The molecules have been around for decades. Watch the pipes.
